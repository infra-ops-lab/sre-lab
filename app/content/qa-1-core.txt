## How a senior engineer answers | Framework
Q: What structure should every troubleshooting answer follow?
A: Use the same 6 steps every time. Interviewers score the method more than the final answer.
- **1. Clarify:** what's broken, for whom, since when, what changed? ("Is it all users or one region? Did anything deploy in the last hour?")
- **2. Impact and mitigation first:** how bad is it for users? Can I stop the bleeding now (roll back, fail over, scale up, shed load) before I know the root cause?
- **3. Narrow down with data:** start from the user-facing symptom and walk the request path (DNS → LB → ingress → service → pod → node → dependency). Use metrics first, then logs, then traces.
- **4. Hypothesis → test → eliminate:** say each hypothesis out loud and the one command that would confirm or kill it.
- **5. Fix:** the smallest safe change; verify against the same signal that showed the problem.
- **6. Prevent:** postmortem, alert that would have caught it earlier, automation or guardrail so it can't repeat.
F: What would you check first, and why that before anything else? What if your first hypothesis is wrong?

Q: How do you answer a design question as an SRE (not as a developer)?
A: A developer designs features; an SRE designs for failure, scale and operability.
- **Requirements:** users, traffic (QPS, peak vs average), data size, latency and availability target (the SLO).
- **Estimate:** rough numbers out loud (requests/sec, storage/day, machines). Round freely; show the maths.
- **High-level design:** boxes and arrows, then walk one request through it.
- **Failure modes:** for each box: what if it dies, slows down, or gets 10x traffic? Blast radius?
- **Operability:** how you monitor it (SLIs), deploy it safely, scale it, back it up and restore it.
- **Trade-offs:** name 2 options, pick one, say what it costs.
F: Where is the single point of failure? What breaks first at 10x traffic?

Q: How do you answer "tell me about a time…" (behavioural) questions?
A: STAR-L, about 2-3 minutes:
- **Situation** (2 lines), **Task** (your responsibility), **Actions** (3 things *you* did; say "I", not "we"), **Result** (with numbers), **Learning** (what you do differently now).
- Pick stories with a conflict or hard decision in them; "everything went fine" stories score low.
- Have 8 stories ready; most questions map onto one of them.
F: What would you do differently? What did your manager think?

Q: What do you do when you don't know the answer?
A: Never bluff. Senior interviewers probe until they hit the edge of your knowledge, on purpose.
- Say it plainly: "I haven't run that in production."
- Then reason from first principles: "but here's how I'd approach it: …" and connect to something you have done.
- Example: "I haven't operated Postgres in production, but I've debugged etcd and Ceph latency, so I'd start with disk latency, connection counts and slow queries."
F: (They usually move on. Recovering calmly scores better than a weak guess.)

## SRE fundamentals | SRE
Q: What is SRE, and how is it different from DevOps?
A: DevOps is a culture: developers and ops share ownership and automate delivery. SRE is one concrete way to implement it, with engineering rules attached.
- **SRE = treat operations as a software problem.** Reliability is a feature, measured with SLIs/SLOs.
- **Error budget** decides the balance between shipping features and reliability work. That's the key difference: it turns "is it reliable enough?" from opinion into a number.
- **Toil cap:** SREs spend at most ~50% of time on ops work; the rest goes to automation and engineering.
- **Blameless postmortems**, **eliminating toil**, **capacity planning**, **safe change** (most outages come from changes).
F: If you joined a team doing "DevOps", what would you change first to make it SRE?

Q: What is toil? How do you reduce it?
A: Toil is work that is **manual, repetitive, automatable, reactive, has no lasting value, and grows linearly with service size**.
- Examples: manual cert renewals, hand-run upgrades per site, restarting pods by hand, answering the same ticket.
- **Measure it first:** track hours per category for 2-4 weeks (tickets, on-call pages).
- **Kill the biggest first:** automate (scripts → self-service → fully automatic), or remove the need (fix the root cause).
- Not toil: on-call for real incidents, design work, writing postmortems.
F: Give a toil example you eliminated and the hours saved.

Q: What does "reliability" actually mean? Is 100% availability the goal?
A: Reliability is "the system does what users expect, when they expect it". **100% is the wrong target.**
- Users can't tell 99.99% from 100% (their own Wi-Fi and phone fail more often).
- Each extra nine costs about 10x more and slows feature delivery.
- Pick the target from user needs and business cost; the gap between the target and 100% is the **error budget** you spend on releases and experiments.
F: Who decides the SLO? What if product wants 99.999%?

Q: What is an error budget policy?
A: A written agreement (signed by eng + product) on what happens as the budget is spent:
- **Budget healthy:** ship normally, take risks, run experiments.
- **Budget burning fast:** slow releases, add review for risky changes.
- **Budget exhausted:** freeze feature releases (only reliability fixes and security patches) until the budget recovers over the window; top incident causes go to the top of the backlog.
- **Escalation path** if teams disagree (e.g., VP decides).
- It's not punishment; it's an automatic, pre-agreed decision so nobody argues during a crisis.
F: Product says the freeze will miss a big launch. What do you do?

Q: What is the difference between an SLI, SLO and SLA?
A:
- **SLI (indicator):** a measurement of user experience. Ratio of good events to total events, e.g. "% of HTTP requests that returned non-5xx in under 300 ms".
- **SLO (objective):** the target for an SLI over a window, e.g. "99.9% over 30 days". Internal.
- **SLA (agreement):** a contract with customers with penalties (credits). Always **looser** than the SLO so you get warned before you owe money (e.g., SLO 99.9%, SLA 99.5%).
F: Why must the SLA be looser than the SLO?

## SLOs and error budgets | SLO
Q: Define SLIs and SLOs for a customer-facing REST API.
A: Pick 2-3 SLIs that match what users feel; measure them at the load balancer or ingress (closest to the user).
- **Availability:** good = responses that are not 5xx (and not timeouts) / all valid requests. SLO e.g. **99.9% over 28-30 days**.
- **Latency:** good = requests served in under X ms. Use a threshold, not an average. e.g. **99% < 300 ms, 99.9% < 1 s**.
- Exclude 4xx caused by the client (bad requests), but watch 429s if you throttle legitimate users.
- Optional: **correctness** (right data) or **freshness** for async pipelines.
- Write down how it's measured (which metric, which query), and review the target quarterly.
F: Why measure at the load balancer, not in the app? How would you handle a big customer whose traffic dominates the ratio?

Q: 99.9% availability over 30 days: how much downtime is allowed? And 99.99%?
A: 30 days = 43,200 minutes.
- **99%** → 432 min ≈ **7.2 hours**
- **99.5%** → 216 min ≈ **3.6 hours**
- **99.9%** → 43.2 min
- **99.95%** → 21.6 min
- **99.99%** → 4.32 min
- For request-based SLOs it's a count: at 99.9% and 10M requests/month you may fail **10,000 requests**.
F: With 4 minutes a month (99.99%), can a human even respond in time? What does that force you to build?

Q: What is burn rate, and how do you alert on SLOs?
A: **Burn rate = how fast you're spending the budget relative to the allowed pace.** Burn rate 1 uses exactly the whole budget by the end of the window; burn rate 10 uses it in a tenth of the time.
- Don't alert on "error rate > X%" for 1 minute (too noisy), and don't wait until the budget is gone (too late).
- **Multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerts** (Google SRE Workbook), for a 30-day 99.9% SLO:
- **Page:** burn rate **14.4** over 1 h (and over 5 min to confirm it's still happening) → 2% of the monthly budget in an hour.
- **Page:** burn rate **6** over 6 h (and 30 min) → 5% in 6 hours.
- **Ticket:** burn rate **1** over 3 days (and 6 h) → 10% in 3 days, slow leak.
- The short window makes the alert reset quickly once the problem is fixed.
F: Why two windows per alert? What's the detection time for a total outage with the 14.4x rule?

Q: A brand-new service has no traffic history. How do you set its SLO?
A:
- Start from **user expectations and dependencies**: you can't be more available than what you depend on (if the DB is 99.95%, you won't hit 99.99%).
- Set a **provisional SLO** a bit below what you think you can do, measure for 2-4 weeks, then tighten or loosen.
- Don't attach a strict error-budget policy until the SLI itself is trustworthy.
- Review after the first few incidents: did the SLO burn when users actually complained? If not, the SLI is wrong.
F: How do you calculate the availability of a service that calls 3 dependencies in series?

Q: What SLIs would you define for a Kubernetes platform (not an app)?
A: Users of a platform are developers and their workloads. Measure what they feel:
- **API server availability:** % of kube-apiserver requests that are non-5xx, and latency (p99 of mutating calls < 1 s).
- **Pod start time:** % of pods Ready within N seconds of creation (scheduling + image pull + start).
- **Deploy success:** % of rollouts that finish successfully without platform-caused failure.
- **DNS:** % of CoreDNS queries answered successfully under 50 ms.
- **Node health:** % of nodes Ready (capacity available).
- **Upgrade success rate** for fleet upgrades.
F: How would you measure these across 1,000+ clusters?

Q: The error budget is exhausted but the team needs to ship a big feature. What do you do?
A:
- Go back to the **error budget policy**; if there isn't one, this is why you need one.
- Look at **what burned the budget**: if it was a platform/dependency outage outside the team's control, the policy may exempt it.
- Options: ship behind a **feature flag** to a small % with a fast rollback; or a one-time exception signed by the right leader, with reliability work committed right after.
- Never silently ignore it; then the SLO means nothing.
F: Who has the final say?

Q: Why use percentiles instead of averages for latency?
A: Averages hide the tail. 1% of requests at 10 s barely moves the average, but those users are furious, and at scale 1% is thousands of people.
- Use **p50** (typical user), **p95/p99** (tail).
- One page load fans out to many backend calls, so the backend p99 is what many users see.
- In Prometheus, compute percentiles from **histograms** (`histogram_quantile`), which can be aggregated across pods; summaries can't be aggregated.
F: Can you average p99s across pods? (No. Aggregate the histogram buckets first, then take the quantile.)

Q: How do you introduce SLOs in a team that has never had them?
A:
- Start with **one critical user journey** (e.g., login or checkout), not every service.
- Measure the SLI for a few weeks; set the SLO near the current performance.
- Replace noisy threshold alerts with **burn-rate alerts** for that SLO; show the reduction in pages.
- Review the SLO dashboard in a weekly ops meeting; agree the error budget policy with product.
- Expand to the next journeys once people see value.
F: How do you convince a manager who thinks SLOs are bureaucracy?

## Observability and alerting | Obs
Q: What are the four golden signals? What are RED and USE?
A:
- **Golden signals (Google):** **latency, traffic, errors, saturation**. For user-facing services.
- **RED (for services):** **Rate, Errors, Duration**.
- **USE (for resources: CPU, memory, disk, network):** **Utilization, Saturation, Errors** (Brendan Gregg).
- Use RED on every service dashboard, USE on every node/resource dashboard; together they cover "is the user hurting?" and "which resource is the bottleneck?"
F: What's the difference between utilization and saturation? (Utilization = how busy, e.g. CPU 80%. Saturation = work waiting, e.g. run queue length, I/O queue.)

Q: What makes a good alert? How do you fix alert fatigue?
A: **Every page must be urgent, actionable, and about user impact.**
- **Page** only for symptoms users feel (SLO burn, total outage). **Ticket** for things that can wait (disk 80% in 3 days). **Dashboard only** for everything else.
- Each alert links a **runbook**: what it means, how to check, how to mitigate.
- **Fix fatigue:** list every alert from the last 30 days with how often it fired and whether anyone acted. Delete or downgrade alerts nobody acts on; merge duplicates; replace cause-based alerts (CPU > 90%) with SLO burn alerts; add `for:` durations and inhibition.
- Track **pages per on-call shift**; target about 2 or fewer real incidents per shift.
F: CPU is 95% but users are fine. Should that page? (No, at most a ticket for capacity.)

Q: Metrics, logs and traces: when do you use which?
A:
- **Metrics:** cheap, aggregated numbers over time. Detect and alert ("something is wrong, how much").
- **Logs:** detailed events. Explain a specific failure ("why did this request fail").
- **Traces:** one request across many services. Find *where* the time or error is in a distributed call path.
- Typical flow: alert on a metric → find the slow service with traces → read its logs for the exact error.
- Link them: put the **trace ID in every log line** (exemplars connect metrics to traces).
F: Logging costs are exploding. What do you do? (Sample, drop debug logs in prod, shorter retention, structured logs, metrics instead of logs for counts.)

Q: What is high cardinality in Prometheus and why is it dangerous?
A: Cardinality = number of unique time series = every unique combination of label values.
- A label like `user_id`, `request_id`, full URL path or pod IP can create millions of series.
- Each series uses memory in Prometheus; it slows queries and can crash (OOM) the server.
- **Fix:** never put unbounded values in labels; normalise paths (`/user/:id`); drop labels with `metric_relabel_configs`; find the culprits with `topk(10, count by (__name__)({__name__=~".+"}))` or the TSDB status page.
F: How do you find which metric is exploding? How do you protect Prometheus from one bad team? (Sample limits per scrape: `sample_limit`.)

Q: Counter vs gauge vs histogram vs summary?
A:
- **Counter:** only goes up (resets on restart). Requests, errors. Always use `rate()` or `increase()`.
- **Gauge:** goes up and down. Memory, queue length, temperature.
- **Histogram:** counts observations in buckets (e.g., latency). Aggregatable across instances; compute percentiles with `histogram_quantile()`.
- **Summary:** computes quantiles inside the client. Accurate but **cannot be aggregated** across pods.
F: Why can't you just `rate()` a gauge?

Q: How would you scale Prometheus for many clusters?
A:
- One Prometheus per cluster (scrapes locally, keeps alerting local and working if the network fails).
- **Global view:** Thanos or Cortex/Mimir: sidecar or remote-write ships data to object storage; a global query layer queries all clusters; long retention in cheap storage.
- **HA:** run 2 Prometheus replicas per cluster; deduplicate at the query layer; Alertmanager in a cluster (gossip) deduplicates alerts.
- Federation only for a small set of aggregated metrics.
- Keep cardinality budgets per team.
F: The link to the central site is down. Do alerts still work? (Yes, if alerting is evaluated locally in each cluster. That's why.)

Q: Who monitors the monitoring?
A:
- A **dead man's switch / watchdog alert** that always fires; an external service (or a second Alertmanager) pages you when it *stops* arriving.
- Alert on Prometheus itself: scrape failures (`up == 0`), rule evaluation failures, TSDB errors, high memory.
- Synthetic checks from **outside** your infrastructure (blackbox exporter or an external uptime service) so you see what users see.
F: Your whole monitoring stack is down during an incident. What do you do?

Q: What is OpenTelemetry and why does it matter?
A: An open, vendor-neutral standard (CNCF) for producing traces, metrics and logs.
- SDKs and auto-instrumentation in apps; the **OTel Collector** receives, processes (batch, sample, drop attributes) and exports to any backend (Prometheus, Jaeger/Tempo, Elastic, vendors).
- Benefit: instrument once, switch backends without code changes; consistent context (trace IDs) across services.
- **Sampling:** head sampling (decide at the start, cheap) vs tail sampling (decide after seeing the whole trace, keeps errors and slow traces, needs more memory).
F: How do you keep tracing costs down?

## Incident management | Incident
Q: Error rates are rising and no customer has complained yet. Walk me through what you do.
A:
- **Acknowledge and assess:** which SLI, how fast is it burning, which users/regions? Declare an incident early if impact is real; it's cheap to downgrade later.
- **What changed?** Recent deploys, config changes, cert expiry, traffic spike, dependency status pages.
- **Mitigate first:** roll back the recent change, fail over, scale out, or shed non-critical load. Restoring service beats understanding it.
- **Narrow down:** RED dashboard per service along the request path; traces for slow/failed requests; logs for the exact error.
- **Communicate:** status updates on a fixed cadence (e.g., every 30 min) to stakeholders, even if "no news".
- **Close:** confirm the SLI is back to normal, then postmortem.
F: The recent deploy can't be rolled back (DB migration). Now what?

Q: What does an Incident Commander do?
A: The IC **coordinates, not fixes**. Roles in a big incident:
- **Incident Commander:** owns the incident: sets priorities, assigns tasks, decides (roll back or not), keeps the timeline, calls for help.
- **Operations / tech lead:** hands on keyboard, debugging and fixing.
- **Communications lead:** status page, stakeholder and customer updates.
- **Scribe:** timeline of actions and findings (feeds the postmortem).
- For small incidents one person may hold several roles; hand over explicitly ("I'm handing IC to Ravi").
F: Two senior engineers disagree on the fix during the incident. What do you do as IC?

Q: How do you define incident severity levels?
A: By **user/business impact**, not by how hard it is technically. Example:
- **SEV1:** most users can't use a core feature, data loss, security breach. All hands, exec comms, status page.
- **SEV2:** significant degradation or a subset of customers down. On-call + team, regular updates.
- **SEV3:** minor impact, workaround exists. Fix in business hours.
- **SEV4:** no user impact (e.g., one redundant node lost).
- Write the criteria down so on-call doesn't have to judge under stress.
F: Who can declare and downgrade a SEV1?

Q: What makes a good postmortem?
A:
- **Blameless:** focus on systems and processes. People made reasonable decisions with the information they had; ask "why did the system allow this?"
- **Contents:** summary, impact (users, duration, SLO budget used), timeline, root cause and contributing factors, what went well, what went badly, where we got lucky, **action items with owners and due dates**.
- Action items in the backlog with priority; review completion monthly.
- Share widely; patterns across postmortems (e.g., 40% from config changes) drive investment.
F: Action items never get done. How do you fix that?

Q: Is "5 whys" a good root-cause method?
A: Useful, but limited.
- Good: forces you past the first answer ("the pod crashed") to systemic causes ("no memory limit review in the deploy process").
- Limits: real incidents usually have **several contributing factors**, not one root cause; 5 whys follows one chain and can end at "human error".
- Better: list contributing factors across categories (detection, change process, design, testing, response) and ask what would have prevented, detected faster, or reduced impact.
F: The postmortem says "root cause: engineer ran the wrong command". What's wrong with that?

Q: Mitigation vs root cause: why mitigate first?
A: Users care about service being back, not about your diagnosis.
- Every minute of investigation while users are down is budget burned.
- Most incidents follow a change, so **rollback** is often both the fastest fix and the first test of the hypothesis.
- Before you mitigate, capture evidence quickly (logs, a heap dump, `kubectl describe` output) so the root cause can still be found later.
F: When would you NOT roll back first? (When rollback is riskier than the problem: irreversible schema change, data corruption spreading. Then stop the spread first, e.g. take writes offline.)

Q: How do you keep on-call healthy and sustainable?
A:
- Measure it: pages per shift, night pages, time to acknowledge, % actionable.
- Rotation of 6-8 people per primary rotation so each is on call about 1 week in 6-8; follow-the-sun if possible.
- Every page gets reviewed weekly: fix or delete noisy alerts.
- Runbooks for every alert; good handover notes between shifts.
- Compensate on-call (time off or pay); protect the next day after a bad night.
F: One service causes 70% of pages. What do you do? (Error budget policy: that team spends its time on reliability until it improves.)

Q: Communication during an incident: what do you send, and how often?
A:
- **First message within minutes:** what's impacted, who's working on it, when the next update is.
- **Regular cadence** (every 15-30 min for SEV1) even with no news: "still investigating, rolled back X, error rate down 50%".
- Separate channels: a war-room channel for responders; a status channel for stakeholders (no debugging chatter).
- External status page for customers if users are affected; plain language, no blame.
- Final message: resolved, impact summary, postmortem to follow.
F: A VP keeps interrupting the war room asking for updates. What do you do?
